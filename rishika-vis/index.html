Who Gets the Sun First?

Every June, a deck of low cloud called the marine layer parks off the California coast and creeps ashore overnight. Locals call it "June Gloom." Here we use hourly GOES-18 satellite scans to watch it move, and to ask whether San Francisco or San Diego actually gets more of it.

Prototype notes (Rishika's section)

What have we done so far?

I turned the raw GOES-18 Clear Sky Mask cube (450 hourly scans of California for June 2025) into an interactive "view from space" map. A Python script regrids it to a 0.03° grid and bit-packs every frame, so the whole month loads in the browser as a single 3 MB file. Readers can scrub or play through the hours from 10 PM to noon, switch between the June average and any single day, and hover the map to read cloud frequency at any point. A linked line chart below the map tracks the same coastal-land boxes we use elsewhere in the article, and clicking it moves the map to that hour. While building this I found that the hour field in our shared JSON is in UTC, not PDT, so I relabeled my two static charts; the data actually covers 1 AM–noon local time, not 8 AM–7 PM.

What will be most challenging to design, and why?

The hardest part will be being honest about the night. Before about 5:45 AM, the satellite only has infrared channels, and low fog looks a lot like the ocean, so some of the biggest swings in the data could be sensor artifacts rather than weather. We need an encoding (shading, annotation, or a confidence band) that tells readers this without burying the story. A second challenge is that our data stops at noon PDT, so any part of the article about afternoon clearing or sunsets will need new downloads of the afternoon scans. Finally, we have to stitch four individual sections into one article that follows an and-but-therefore arc, which means agreeing on shared colors, city definitions, and a single takeaway before the final submission.
