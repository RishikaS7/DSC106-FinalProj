Who Gets the Sun First?

Every June, a gray lid of cloud slides over the California coast. We followed it hour by hour from space to find out whether San Francisco or San Diego really gets more "June Gloom."

By Rishika Sahu, Natalie Wu, Marcus de Ramos and Yagnasri Chilukuri

Ask anyone who has spent a June in California and they will tell you the same thing: the beach is gray in the morning. Tourists expect sunshine. Locals have a name for what they actually get instead: June Gloom. The usual story says it is a Northern California problem, the fog rolling in under the Golden Gate Bridge. But San Diegans swear their mornings are just as gray. So which city actually spends more of June under cloud, and when does each one finally see the sun?

01The view from space

A cloud the size of a state, parked just offshore

The gloom has a technical name: the marine layer. Cold ocean water chills the air sitting right on top of it, and a warm layer of air higher up acts like a lid, trapping that cool, moist air near the surface. The result is a flat sheet of low stratus cloud that can stretch hundreds of miles out to sea.

NOAA's GOES-18 weather satellite photographs the West Coast every few minutes and labels each 2-km pixel as cloudy or clear. We took one scan every hour in June 2025, from 10 PM through noon the next day. Press play below and watch where the cloud lives.

Three things jump out. First, the ocean is almost always white. Out past the coastline, the stratus deck sits there nearly every hour of every day. Second, inland California is almost always clear: the marine layer is shallow, and the coastal mountains stop it cold. The whole fight happens in a thin strip along the shore, which is exactly where people live.

Third, the average hides a lot. On the , only about a quarter to a third of each city's coast is under cloud, a gentle-looking gloom. But individual days are rarely average. On , a swirl of cloud filled the Southern California Bight and buried 91% of San Diego's coast, versus 58% of San Francisco's. One hour later, , San Francisco's coast was completely clear. San Diego's was still mostly gray.

Look at the line chart under the map with June average selected and the pattern holds: after sunrise, San Diego stays a few points cloudier than San Francisco at every hour until noon. The gloom is not a Bay Area problem. If anything, it is San Diego's.

One caution: the shaded hours before sunrise (about 5:45 AM) are harder to trust. At night the satellite cannot use visible light, and low fog looks a lot like the cold ocean in infrared, so those readings swing more than the real clouds probably do. 03Day by day

Which city is actually cloudier day to day?

Looking at the monthly average does not tell us what happened on each day. At noon, San Francisco was cloudier on 18 days, while San Diego was cloudier on 10 days. The other two days were tied. San Diego still had a higher average cloud cover at noon: 21.4% compared with 20.7% in San Francisco. The average also depends on how big the differences were.

Prototype notes

What have we done so far?

Rishika · The view from space

I turned the raw GOES-18 Clear Sky Mask data (450 hourly satellite scans of California for June 2025) into the interactive map in Part 1. A Python script (rishika-vis/prepare_map_data.py) regrids the satellite pixels onto a regular 0.03° latitude-longitude grid and bit-packs every frame, so the whole month loads in the browser as a single 3 MB file. Readers can play or scrub through the hours from 10 PM to noon, switch between the June average and any single day, and hover anywhere on the map to read how often it was cloudy. A linked line chart tracks the same coastal-land boxes the rest of our article uses, and clicking it moves the map to that hour, while highlighted phrases in the text jump the map to specific moments worth seeing. While building this, I found that the hour field in our shared JSON is in UTC, not Pacific time, which means our data actually covers 1 AM to noon local time rather than 8 AM to 7 PM.

Marcus · Day by day

I made a bar chart that compares cloud cover in San Francisco and San Diego for each day in June. You can move the slider or press play to see how the differences change from 6 AM to noon. Hovering over a day shows the cloud cover for both cities and how far apart they were. I also added a count of how many days each city was cloudier so it is easier to compare the daily results with the monthly average.

What will be most challenging to design, and why?

Rishika · The view from space

The hardest design problem is being honest about the night. Before about 5:45 AM, the satellite can only use infrared channels, and low fog looks a lot like the cold ocean, so some of the biggest swings in our data are probably sensor artifacts rather than real weather. We need an encoding, whether shading, annotation, or a confidence band, that tells readers this without burying the story. A second challenge is that our data stops at noon, so any part of the article about afternoon clearing or sunsets will need new downloads of the afternoon scans. Finally, we have to stitch four separately built sections into one article that follows an and-but-therefore arc, which means agreeing on shared colors, city definitions, and a single takeaway before the final deadline.

Marcus · Day by day

I think the hardest part will be explaining why a city can have a higher average cloud cover but still be cloudier on fewer days. The bars help show this because you can see both which city was cloudier and how big the difference was. Another challenge is making all 30 days easy to read, especially on a smaller screen. We also need to make sure our sections use the same time zone and study areas so we are making fair comparisons.
